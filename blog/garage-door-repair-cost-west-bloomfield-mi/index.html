If your garage door has stopped working the way it should, the first question most homeowners ask is a simple one: what is this going to cost to fix? The answer depends heavily on what actually failed. A snapped spring is a different job than a dented panel or an opener that keeps reversing for no reason. This guide walks through the typical price ranges for every common garage door repair in West Bloomfield, along with what moves those numbers up or down and when you are better off replacing the door entirely.

One thing worth noting up front: Michigan and the broader Midwest tend to come in at the lower end of national cost guides. If you have seen pricing on a large home improvement website, local figures in West Bloomfield are usually 10 to 15 percent below those ranges.

What Most Garage Door Repairs Actually Cost

For the most common repairs — springs, cables, rollers, sensors, and off-track issues — most homeowners in West Bloomfield spend somewhere between $100 and $400. That range covers parts and labor for a single repair on a standard residential door. More involved work, like a full opener repair or significant panel damage, typically falls in the $300 to $800 range. Full door replacement with professional installation runs $800 to $2,500 depending on door size, material, and style.

Most technicians charge a service call or diagnostic fee of $50 to $75 to come out and assess the problem. Reputable companies typically roll that into the job cost once you approve the work, so if you proceed with the repair, you are not paying it twice.

Garage Door Repair Costs by Type

Here is what you can expect to pay for each major repair type, based on current parts and labor rates in the West Bloomfield area.

Spring Replacement: $150–$450

Broken springs are the most common garage door repair. Torsion springs — the kind mounted on the rod above the door — typically run $150 to $350 per spring, including labor. Most technicians will strongly recommend replacing both springs at the same time, even if only one has broken, because when one goes the other is usually close behind. For a pair, budget $200 to $450. High-cycle springs rated for 25,000 to 50,000 cycles (instead of the standard 10,000-cycle variety) add $20 to $60 but last significantly longer. Extension springs, which run along the sides of the door, are usually $100 to $200 for the pair. If your door made a loud bang and now will not open, there is a good chance a spring has snapped. Our post on how to tell if your garage door spring is about to break covers the warning signs to watch for before you get to that point.

Cable Repair: $100–$250

Garage door cables work alongside the springs to control the door's movement and keep it tracking evenly. When a cable snaps or frays, the door can drop unevenly or refuse to open at all. Cable repair typically runs $100 to $200. If the broken cable damaged a drum or pulley in the process, add another $50 to $100 for those parts. Cable repairs and spring replacements often go hand in hand — if one cable has failed, a technician will usually inspect the other cable and the springs during the same visit.

Opener Repair: $75–$500

Opener repairs cover a wide range depending on what failed. Minor jobs like safety sensor alignment or limit switch adjustment run $50 to $150. Mid-range repairs — a broken drive gear, trolley replacement, or logic board issue — typically cost $100 to $300. A full motor replacement or new opener installed by a professional runs $200 to $500. As a general rule, if your opener is over 10 to 12 years old and needs a significant repair, a new opener often makes more financial sense than putting money into an aging motor. Our opener troubleshooting guide can help you narrow down what is actually wrong before you call.

Off-Track Door Repair: $125–$225

A door that has jumped its tracks needs immediate attention — it either will not move or is hanging at an angle and cannot be safely forced back by hand. Off-track repair typically runs $125 to $225. If the tracks themselves are bent or damaged (not just the door having slipped out), expect to add $75 to $150 for track straightening or replacement. Attempting to push an off-track door back into position yourself can be genuinely dangerous given the spring tension involved.

Roller Replacement: $90–$200

Worn or cracked rollers are a common cause of grinding, squealing, and rough operation. A full set of replacement rollers typically costs $90 to $200 installed. Upgrading from standard steel rollers to nylon-coated ones costs a few dollars more per roller but runs considerably quieter — worth considering if the noise has been bothering you. Rollers are also often replaced as part of a broader maintenance and tune-up service, which makes it a good time to address them before they fail completely.

Panel Repair or Replacement: $250–$800

A single damaged panel — from a car backing into it or cracking after a hard freeze — typically costs $250 to $800 to replace, including labor. The main variable is whether matching panels are still in production for your door model. If your door is discontinued, sourcing matching panels can be difficult or costly. More than two or three damaged panels, or any damage to the bottom frame, and full door replacement usually becomes the more practical option both for cost and appearance. Panel and dent repair can sometimes address minor cosmetic damage at a lower cost if the structural integrity is still sound.

Sensor Repair and Alignment: $50–$150

The safety sensors near the base of your door tracks stop the door from closing on a person, pet, or object. When they get knocked out of alignment, the door will reverse every time you try to close it — which looks alarming but is often a quick fix. Realignment typically costs $50 to $100. If a sensor is actually faulty and needs replacing, that runs $75 to $150. You can read more about how they work and what can go wrong on our sensor repair page.

Weather Seal Replacement: $75–$175

The rubber seal along the bottom of your garage door keeps out drafts, water, rodents, and cold — which matters significantly during Michigan winters. Bottom seal replacement typically runs $75 to $175, depending on door width and seal type. If the side and top seals (door stop weatherstripping) are worn too, adding those usually costs another $50 to $100. This is one of the more cost-effective repairs on the list and one that tends to pay back quickly in energy savings over a Michigan winter.

Track Repair: $100–$300

Bent, misaligned, or damaged tracks prevent the door from running smoothly and can eventually cause it to come off track entirely. Track repair or realignment typically costs $100 to $300. Severely damaged tracks that need full replacement will run higher and may require a few hours of labor. In most cases, bent tracks can be straightened rather than replaced, which keeps the cost toward the lower end of that range.

What Pushes the Price Up or Down

A few factors consistently affect what you will pay, regardless of the repair type.

Door size. Double-car garage doors have more components, require more time, and use heavier hardware than single-car doors. Springs and cables are sized and priced accordingly — a torsion spring for a two-car door typically costs more than one for a single-car door.

Brand and model. Parts for widely distributed brands like LiftMaster, Chamberlain, and Genie are readily available and reasonably priced. Older or less common models can require more sourcing time, which can add to the final bill.

How long the problem has been ignored. A door that has been grinding or struggling for months often has secondary damage by the time someone calls — worn rollers that have damaged the tracks, or a cable that has frayed and taken out a drum. Catching problems early generally keeps repair costs lower. Our garage door maintenance checklist is a useful reference for what to inspect on a regular basis.

Combining repairs. If a technician is already on-site and finds two or three things that need attention, handling them in one visit is usually less expensive per repair than scheduling separate calls. It is also worth asking about a full tune-up at the same time — it covers lubrication, hardware tightening, and a full safety check.

Same-Day and Emergency Service Pricing

For standard repairs during regular business hours, same-day service should not add to your cost. After-hours or weekend emergency calls — where a broken spring has your car stuck in the garage on a Sunday morning — typically carry an additional fee of $50 to $100. For most people dealing with an emergency garage door situation, that is a reasonable trade-off. When a Repair Is Not the Right Move

Sometimes the numbers just do not add up for a repair. A commonly used benchmark is the 50% rule: if the repair cost approaches half what a new door would cost installed, replacement often makes more financial sense — especially on an older door. That is because a repair fixes one component, while a new door resets the entire system with fresh hardware and a warranty on everything.
