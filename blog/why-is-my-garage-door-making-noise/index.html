Why Is My Garage Door Making That Noise? A Sound-by-Sound Guide

Squeaking, grinding, rattling, banging — each sound tends to point to a different component. Here's how to figure out what yours is telling you.

Home / Blog / Why Is My Garage Door Making That Noise?

Garage doors have more moving parts than most people realize. Every time yours opens or closes, it's cycling springs, cables, rollers, hinges, tracks, and the opener motor all at once. When any one of them starts to wear, struggle, or fail, it usually makes a sound first. Different sounds tend to point to different problems — knowing which is which helps you decide whether to grab a can of lubricant or call a professional.

Squeaking or Squealing

A squeaking garage door is almost always a lubrication problem. The most common culprits are the rollers and the hinges — when those metal parts run dry, they produce a high-pitched squeal that gets worse over time. The fix is simple: spray white lithium grease or silicone lubricant on the rollers, hinges, and the springs. Avoid WD-40. Despite being everyone's first instinct, major door manufacturers like Clopay specifically advise against it because it's a solvent, not a lubricant — it cleans off the grease that's already there and leaves the metal unprotected.

If you lubricate everything and the squeaking comes back within a few days, the rollers themselves may be worn out. Nylon rollers wear down over the years and can develop a wobble that no amount of lube will fix. Roller replacement is a straightforward job and usually eliminates squeaking for good.

Grinding

Grinding is a heavier, lower-pitched sound than squeaking, and it can come from two different places depending on where you hear it.

If the grinding sound comes from the tracks as the door moves, you're likely dealing with debris inside the track, bent track sections, or rollers that are out of alignment. Dirt, leaves, and small stones can work their way into the track over time and cause the door to grind as it rolls over them. A bent section will do the same, but it won't clean away — that usually requires track repair or realignment.

If the grinding is coming from the motor unit on the ceiling, the issue is more likely inside the opener itself. Older openers use a plastic drive gear that meshes with a metal worm gear on the motor. When that plastic gear wears down, you'll hear grinding as the teeth slip. Wayne Dalton's noise troubleshooting guide identifies this gear failure as one of the most common mechanical causes of opener noise. The door may still move, but with each cycle it's grinding through that gear a little more. Schedule opener repair sooner rather than later — a worn gear left alone often means a locked-up motor unit.

Rattling

A rattling garage door is usually the easiest problem on this list to address. The sound almost always means something has come loose — a bolt, a nut, a bracket, or a hinge screw. Garage doors operate with a lot of vibration over thousands of cycles, and hardware gradually works itself loose.

Walk down both sides of the door while it's closed and check every bolt and nut you can see. A socket wrench (a 3/8-inch or 7/16-inch socket covers most of them) is all you need. Snug them up, but don't overtighten — stripped bolts in the door panels are harder to fix than a loose one. Give the brackets and hinges a visual check too; a cracked hinge plate will rattle and eventually fail.

Banging or a Loud Pop

This is the one you don't want to hear. A loud bang or pop — especially one that happens suddenly and is followed by the door behaving strangely (sagging, opening unevenly, or not opening at all) — often means a torsion spring has broken.

A broken spring is a safety issue, not a DIY fix. The springs on your garage door are under enormous tension, and working on them without the right training and tools can cause serious injury. If you suspect a broken spring, stop using the door and call a professional. We cover the full list of warning signs in our post on how to tell if your garage door spring is about to break — worth reading if you noticed anything off before the bang happened.

Our team handles spring repair and replacement on the same day in most cases. If it happens at an inconvenient time, emergency service is available.

Scraping or Screeching

A scraping sound — like metal dragging on metal — while the door is moving usually means a roller has partially left its track. The roller is still moving the door, but it's rubbing against the inside of the track rather than rolling cleanly inside it.

This can escalate quickly. An off-track door puts uneven stress on the other rollers, the cables, and the bottom bracket, and it can eventually jam completely. If you hear a scraping sound and the door seems to hesitate or move slightly crooked, stop using it and have it looked at. Off-track repair is much simpler (and cheaper) when caught early, before the door comes down fully or a cable snaps.

Humming or Vibrating With No Movement

If your opener hums but the door doesn't move, the motor is running but something is preventing it from driving the door. A few possibilities:

The limit switches tell the opener how far to travel before stopping. If they're set wrong, the opener can reach its stop point before the door is fully open or closed and then refuse to reverse. Adjusting the limits is covered in most opener manuals, and it's one of the few things on this list that's genuinely safe for a homeowner to try.

A blocked or misaligned safety sensor will also cause the opener to hum without closing the door — the sensors sit near the floor on both sides of the door, and if one is tilted, dirty, or has something in front of it, the opener interprets that as an obstruction. Cleaning the lens and realigning the sensors takes a minute and often resolves it.

If neither of those applies, a stripped or broken drive gear inside the opener is another common cause — the motor spins but the gear can't translate that into movement. That's a repair rather than a DIY adjustment.

Why Michigan Winters Amplify All of This

If you've lived through a West Bloomfield winter, you already know that cold weather makes everything mechanical harder to deal with. Garage doors are no exception. Metal contracts in the cold, which tightens tolerances throughout the system and makes existing wear more audible. Lubricants thicken and can stop flowing altogether below certain temperatures, leaving rollers and hinges running dry even if you lubricated them in the fall. Springs become more brittle in extreme cold and are statistically more likely to snap on a frigid January morning when they're already under the strain of contraction.

Our Michigan winter garage door care guide covers the seasonal prep that keeps most of these issues from showing up. One thing worth knowing specifically: the bottom rubber seal on your door can freeze to the garage floor overnight when temperatures drop hard. If you force the door open while the seal is frozen, you'll often hear a tearing or cracking sound and end up with a damaged seal. If the door seems stuck at the bottom in winter, don't force it — break the seal loose gently first. A torn seal leads to drafts, water intrusion, and eventually a need for weather seal replacement.

What You Can Fix Yourself vs. What You Shouldn't

To be direct about it: lubrication and tightening loose hardware are the two things most homeowners can safely handle on their own. They're low-risk, require no specialized tools, and fix a meaningful portion of the noises people call about.

Everything else on this list — springs, cables, tracks, off-track rollers, and anything inside the opener motor unit — is better left to a professional. Springs and cables are under serious tension and can cause significant injury if they release unexpectedly. Tracks that aren't realigned correctly will keep causing problems. And opener internals vary enough by model that guessing at a repair often makes the underlying issue harder to fix afterward.

If you're not sure which category your noise falls into, our FAQ page covers a lot of the common questions, or you can reach out directly and describe what you're hearing. A maintenance tune-up is also worth considering if your door has been making noise for a while and you're not sure what's going on — we inspect the entire system, lubricate everything, and tell you exactly what we find.
